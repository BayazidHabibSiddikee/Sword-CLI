Theory:
A register is a group of flip-flops, each sharing a common clock and capable of storing one bit of
information. An n-bit register consists of a group of n flip-flops capable of storing n bits of binary
information. In addition to the flip-flops, a register may feature combinational gates that perform
specific data-processing tasks. In its broadest definition, a register consists of a group of flip-flops
combined with gates that dictate how information is transferred into the register.
A register capable of shifting its stored binary information to its neighboring cell in a selected
direction is called a shift register. The logical configuration of a shift register consists of a chain
of cascaded flip-flops, with the output of one flip-flop connected to the input of the next. All flip-
flops receive common clock pulses, which activate the shift of data from one stage to the next. [1]
SISO Shift Register:
The SISO shift register is the most basic form of data shifting circuit. It accepts binary data serially
(one bit at a time) through a single input pin and shifts it out serially through a single output pin.
Internally, the output of each flip-flop connects directly to the input of the successive one.
Because data must pass through every single flip-flop in the chain, it requires n clock pulses to
fully load an n-bit word into the register, and another n clock pulses to shift it out completely.
While this makes the SISO register relatively slow for data transfer, it is incredibly useful as a
digital delay line. By adjusting the clock frequency or the number of flip-flops in the cascade,
engineers can introduce a precise time delay into a digital signal, allowing it to act as a
synchronized buffer between asynchronous devices. [2]

SIPO Shift Register:
The SIPO shift register is primarily used as a serial-to-parallel converter. Like the SISO register,
data is fed into the system serially, bit by bit, requiring n clock cycles to load an n-bit word.
However,  the  key  structural  difference  is  that  the  outputs  of  every  flip-flop  are  externally
accessible simultaneously. Once the entire data string has been clocked into the register, the
complete binary word can be read across the parallel output lines all at once. This configuration is
widely used in communication systems (such as UART receivers) where data is transmitted over
a single wire to save space, but needs to be processed by a microprocessor in parallel chunks (like
8-bit bytes) for faster internal computation. [2]

PISO Shift Register:
The PISO shift register functions as a parallel-to-serial converter, performing the exact opposite
role of the SIPO circuit. It takes parallel data from multiple input lines and transmits it out
sequentially over a single output line. To achieve this, the PISO register utilizes a control signal
typically called "Shift/Load". When this control line is set to the "Load" state, combinational logic
(such as multiplexers) bypasses the shifting mechanism, allowing all n bits of input data to be
jammed into the flip-flops simultaneously on a single clock pulse. Once the data is loaded, the
control line is switched to "Shift", and subsequent clock pulses push the stored data out of the final
flip-flop sequentially. This is a critical component in digital transmitters, taking parallel data from
a computer bus and formatting it for transmission over a single-wire network. [2]

PIPO Shift Register:
The PIPO register is the fastest of the four types because it handles all data loading and outputting
simultaneously. It features dedicated parallel input lines (PI) and parallel output lines (PO). In a
PIPO circuit, there is no physical interconnection between the output of one flip-flop and the input
of the next; therefore, no actual "shifting" of data occurs between the internal cells. Instead, upon
the arrival of the clock edge, the data present on the input lines is instantly transferred to the output
lines in a single clock cycle. This configuration acts as a multi-bit data latch, serving as temporary
memory  storage  or  a  synchronization  buffer  holding  data  steady  while  other  parts  of  a
microprocessor complete their operations. [2]

Johnson Counter
A Johnson Counter (also known as a Twisted Ring Counter) is an advanced variation of a shift
register where the inverted output (Q-bar or complemented output) of the final flip-flop is fed back
into the data input of the first flip-flop. Because of this twisted feedback loop, an n-stage Johnson
counter cycles through exactly 2n distinct states before repeating. For example, a 4-bit Johnson
counter will produce 8 unique states. The sequence fills with 1s from left to right, and then empties
with 0s from left to right. This behavior requires significantly less decoding logic than a traditional
binary counter. As a result, Johnson counters are highly favored in hardware design for creating
complex  timing  sequences,  multi-phase  clock  generators,  and  glitch-free  digital  frequency
dividers.

Ring Counter:
A Ring Counter is a straightforward circular shift register where the standard, non-inverted output
(Q) of the last flip-flop is looped directly back to the input of the first flip-flop. Unlike the Johnson
counter, a Ring counter typically requires a pre-set or initialization step where a single logic '1' is
injected into the first flip-flop while the rest are set to '0'. Once the clock starts, this single '1' simply
rotates endlessly through the chain of flip-flops. Because only one output is high at any given
moment, an n-bit ring counter provides n distinct states. This "one-hot" state encoding makes the
Ring Counter exceptionally easy to decode, eliminating the need for external logic gates when
stepping through a specific sequence of operations in a microcontroller or state machine. [2]

Discussion:
In this experiment, different types of shift registers including SISO, SIPO, PISO, and PIPO—along
with Ring Counter and Johnson Counter circuits, were designed and simulated using Logisim. The
shift registers demonstrated various methods of data storage and transfer through serial and parallel
input-output configurations controlled by clock pulses. The SISO and SIPO registers successfully
illustrated the conversion of serial data into serial and parallel outputs, respectively. Conversely,
the PISO and PIPO registers demonstrated the handling of parallel data with serial or parallel
outputs. Additionally, the Ring Counter exhibited the circulation of a single logic state through a
sequence of flip-flops, whereas the Johnson Counter generated a unique counting sequence by
feeding the inverted output of the last flip-flop back to the first. The simulation results for all
circuits were consistent with their theoretical operations, firmly verifying the correctness of the
designs.
Conclusion:
This experiment provided valuable insight into the operation and practical applications of shift
registers and counters in digital electronics. Through Logisim simulation, the principles of data
storage, data shifting, and sequence generation were successfully demonstrated. The behavior of
the SISO, SIPO, PISO, and PIPO shift registers, as well as the Ring and Johnson Counters,
perfectly matched the expected theoretical outcomes. This successfully fulfills the objectives of
the experiment and strengthens our foundational understanding of sequential logic systems.
