Experiment Name: Implementation of Basic Logic Gates Using Integrated Circuits (ICs) on

Objectives:

•  To comprehend the operational principles and truth tables of fundamental digital logic
gates (AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR, XNOR) through practical hardware
implementation.

•  To implement, verify, and analyze the circuit behavior of these basic logic gates utilizing

standard TTL ICs on a prototyping breadboard.

•  To cultivate essential hands-on skills in digital circuit wiring, component integration, and

the troubleshooting of connectivity issues.

•  To bridge the theoretical-practical divide by directly correlating theoretical Boolean logic

design with tangible, physical outputs.

Introduction:

Digital  electronics  form  the  bedrock  of  modern  computing,  communication,  and  control
architectures. At the core of these systems  are logic gates, which  are the fundamental  building
blocks  that  execute  basic  Boolean  operations  required  for  sophisticated  electronic  design.  To
effectively  bridge  theoretical  logic  concepts  with  real-world  applications,  practical  circuit
implementation is strictly necessary.

This experiment investigates the operation of the primary logic gates (7408 (AND), 7432 (OR),
7404  (NOT),  7400  (NAND),  7402  (NOR),  7486(XOR),  74266(XNOR))  using  74-series
Transistor-Transistor Logic (TTL) integrated circuits. The TTL ICs utilized in this lab encapsulate
multiple independent logic gates within a single package. By physically wiring these components
on  a  breadboard,  applying  +5V  DC  regulated  power,  and  monitoring  output  states  via  LED
indicators, theoretical truth tables are empirically verified. This hands-on methodology not only
demonstrates the functional realities of digital logic but also establishes a foundational skill set for
designing  more  complex  architectures  like  arithmetic  logic  units  (ALUs),  multiplexers,  and
memory registers.

In  this  laboratory  experiment,  these  logic  gates  are  implemented  and  tested  on  a  breadboard,
allowing circuit construction without permanent soldering. This hands-on approach helps students
understand the practical operation of logic gates and their role in building larger digital systems
such as multiplexers, adders, and flip-flops. The output of each gate is displayed using an LED
connected  through  a  current-limiting  resistor,  while  a  regulated  +5  V  DC  supply  provides  the
operating  voltage  required  for  TTL  devices.  Jumper  wires  are  used  to  establish  the  necessary
circuit  connections  between  the  ICs,  power  source,  and  ground.  Through  the  successful
implementation  and  testing  of  these  logic  gates,  students  gain  practical  experience  in  circuit
assembly, verification, and troubleshooting. These skills form an essential foundation for advanced
studies in digital electronics, microprocessor systems, and embedded system design.

Working Procedure

1.  Preparation:  All  necessary  hardware,  including  the  breadboard,  specified  TTL  ICs,
resistors,  LEDs,  jumper  wires,  and  the  +5V  DC  power  supply,  was  assembled  and
inspected.

2.  Component  Mounting:  The  selected  logic  ICs  were  firmly  mounted  across  the  central
divider of the breadboard to ensure electrical isolation between the opposing rows of pins.
3.  Power Distribution (Biasing): To activate the logic chips, the VCC and GND terminals of
each  IC  were  wired  directly  to  the  +5V  rail  and  the  common  ground  rail,  respectively,
establishing the necessary operating voltage.

4.  Input Configuration: Push-button switches were integrated into the circuit and connected
to the gate inputs, serving as manual generators to toggle logic states between HIGH and
LOW.

5.  Output Monitoring: LEDs, placed in series with $220~\Omega$ current-limiting resistors,
were connected to the respective output pins of the logic gates to safely visualize the signal
status.

6.  Testing  and  Simulation:  The  input  switches  were  methodically  toggled  to  simulate  all

possible binary combinations outlined in the standard truth tables.

7.  Data Collection and Verification: The resulting output responses for the AND, OR, NOT,
NAND,  NOR,  XOR,  and  XNOR  gates  were  observed  via  the  LEDs,  documented,  and
cross-referenced against theoretical expectations to verify accurate gate functionality.
8.  Teardown:  Following  successful  verification,  the  power  supply  was  isolated,  and  the

breadboard circuit was carefully dismantled.

Circuit Development

1. AND Gate (IC 7408): The AND gate executes the Boolean multiplication operation (Y = A.
B).  It  dictates  that  the  output  will  register  as  HIGH  (1)  strictly  when  all  individual  inputs  are
simultaneously HIGH. Conversely, if any single input is LOW (0), the output is driven LOW. This
logic is widely applied in decision-making circuits where multiple conditions must be met. The IC
7408 serves this function, providing four independent 2-input AND gates in one package.

Figure 6.1: Basic NOR Gate

2. OR Gate (IC 7432): The OR gate performs Boolean addition (Y = A + B). It yields a HIGH
(1) output if at least one of its inputs is actively HIGH. The output drops to LOW (0) only when
every  input  is  LOW.  This  behavior  is  utilized  in  systems  where  independent  conditions  can
individually trigger a shared output response. The IC 7432 is standard for this operation, containing
four 2-input OR gates.

Fig 2: OR Gate

3.  NOT  Gate  (IC  7404):  Commonly  termed  an  inverter,  the  NOT  gate  performs  logical
complementation. Featuring a single input and single output, it simply reverses the logic state: a
HIGH input yields a LOW output, and a LOW input yields a HIGH output. It is heavily relied
upon for signal inversion and logic control. The 7404 IC provides six discrete NOT gates.

Fig 03: NOT Gate

4. NAND Gate (IC 7400): The NAND (NOT-AND) gate is a universal logic element that produces
a LOW (0) output exclusively when  all inputs  are HIGH (1); otherwise, it  defaults to  a HIGH
output.  Functionally,  it  operates  as  an  AND  gate  immediately  followed  by  an  inverter.  Its
"universal" designation stems from the fact that any digital logic circuit can be synthesized using
NAND gates alone. The IC 7400 contains four independent 2-input NAND gates.

Fig 4: NAND Gate

5. NOR Gate (IC 7402): The NOR (NOT-OR) gate is the second universal logic gate, outputting
a HIGH signal solely when all inputs are held LOW. Any presence of a HIGH input immediately
forces the output LOW. Like the NAND gate, it can be configured to replicate the function of any
other digital gate. The IC 7402 facilitates this with four independent 2-input NOR gates.

Fig 5: NOR Gate

6. XOR Gate (IC 7486): The Exclusive-OR (XOR) gate serves as a digital inequality detector. It
generates a HIGH (1) output only when its inputs are at differing logic levels (e.g., one HIGH, one
LOW).  If  the  inputs  match,  the  output  is  LOW.  This  characteristic  makes  it  indispensable  for
binary  arithmetic  addition,  parity  generation,  and  error  detection.  The  7486  IC  contains  four
independent 2-input XOR gates.

Fig 6: XOR Gate

7.XNOR Gate (IC 74266 / IC 4077): The Exclusive-NOR (XNOR) gate acts as an equivalence
or equality detector. It produces a HIGH (1) output when its inputs are identical (both HIGH or
both LOW), and a LOW (0) output when the inputs diverge. Acting as the precise inverse of the
XOR gate, it is highly utilized in data verification and digital comparison systems.

Discussion

Fig 7: XNOR Gate

Throughout this laboratory exercise, the core logic behaviors of AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR,
XOR,  and  XNOR  gates  were  successfully  and  empirically  validated  utilizing  standard  TTL
integrated circuits. The implementation required meticulous attention to breadboard architecture,
specifically regarding proper IC placement across the central trench and accurate pin-to-pin wiring.
The standard 74-series ICs utilized feature durable outer plastic housings with standardized pin
numbering, which greatly streamlined the identification and prototyping process.

By employing tactile push buttons to inject logic signals and LEDs to visually monitor the resulting
output  states,  the  physical  hardware  behavior  was  proven  to  align  flawlessly  with  established
theoretical  Boolean  truth  tables.  The  accuracy  of  these  results  was  highly  dependent  on
maintaining a stable +5V DC power supply, ensuring correct VCC and GND biasing for every
chip, and adhering strictly to the pin diagrams. Ultimately, this exercise underscored the reliability
of TTL ICs as the foundational architecture for complex digital electronics and highlighted the
necessity of systematic troubleshooting in hardware development.

Conclusion

This  experiment  successfully  fulfilled  all  stated  objectives  by  demonstrating  the  operational
dynamics  of  fundamental  digital  logic  gates  through  direct  hardware  implementation.  The
empirical verification of the truth tables for the AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR, and XNOR
gates  confirmed  absolute  alignment  between  theoretical  Boolean  logic  and  physical  circuit
behavior. Furthermore, the hands-on methodology cultivated essential practical engineering skills,
including prototyping, component integration, and systematic troubleshooting, providing a robust
conceptual and practical foundation for future studies in advanced digital system design.
