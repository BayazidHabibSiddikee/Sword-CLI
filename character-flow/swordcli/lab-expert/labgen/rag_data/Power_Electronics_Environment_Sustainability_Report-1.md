Experiment No: 01
Experiment Name: Study the Role of Power Electronics for Environment
and Sustainability

Abstract

Power electronics — the branch of electrical engineering concerned with the conversion and
control of electric power using semiconductor switching devices such as diodes, thyristors,
power MOSFETs, and IGBTs — has become a cornerstone technology for achieving global
energy and environmental sustainability. This report studies the role that power-electronic
converters play in enabling renewable-energy integration, smart-grid operation, electric
transportation, and industrial energy efficiency, thereby reducing greenhouse-gas emissions
and supporting a cleaner energy future. Recent advances in wide-bandgap semiconductors,
particularly silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN), have pushed converter efficiencies
beyond 98 percent, and the global power-electronics market for renewable-energy applications
is projected to grow from roughly USD 16-18 billion in 2025 to more than USD 40 billion by
the mid-2030s.

The discussion combines general concepts with country-specific data for Bangladesh, drawing
on figures published by the International Energy Agency (IEA), the Bangladesh Power
Development Board (BPDB), the Sustainable and Renewable Energy Development Authority
(SREDA), and Power Grid Company of Bangladesh PLC (PGCB), to illustrate the present state
of the power sector, the extent of renewable-energy penetration, and the challenges of meeting
rising demand while the national grid still depends heavily on natural gas, oil, and coal.

Introduction

Power electronics is the application of electronics to the control and conversion of electric
power. Early high-power conversion relied on mercury-arc valves; modern systems instead use
semiconductor switching devices, with variable-speed drive systems ranging in power from a
few hundred watts to tens of megawatts. Because almost every stage of modern power
generation, transmission, and consumption now passes through some form of power-electronic
converter, the technology sits at the centre of the transition toward a more sustainable energy
system.

Climate change and the continuous growth of global electricity demand have made efficient
energy generation, transmission, and utilisation one of the greatest engineering challenges of
this century. Beyond traditional motor drives and uninterruptible power supplies, power
electronics is now essential for integrating renewable sources, electric vehicles, HVDC
transmission, and smart grids, all of which depend on converters for voltage regulation,
frequency control, and grid synchronisation.

Energy sustainability refers to meeting present and future energy demand without exhausting
natural resources, primarily through sources such as solar, wind, geothermal, hydropower, and
ocean energy that are generally available at no fuel cost and can be used repeatedly.
Environmental sustainability, as defined by the United Nations World Commission on
Environment and Development, means acting in a way that ensures future generations retain

access to the natural resources needed for a quality of life at least equal to that of the present
generation,   while   improving   human   welfare   within   the   carrying   capacity   of   the   Earth's
supporting ecosystems.

Key Features of Power Electronics for Sustainability

Power-electronic converters act as the interface that transfers electricity from one stage of the
supply chain to the next while allowing its voltage, frequency, and waveform to be flexibly
controlled. This flexibility underlies several features that make power electronics central to
environmental and energy sustainability:

• Renewable-energy integration: Inverters convert the DC output of solar photovoltaic
panels and battery systems, and back-to-back converters regulate variable-frequency wind
power, into stable grid-compatible AC, allowing intermittent sources to be connected
safely to the grid.

• Wide-bandgap devices: Silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) semiconductors
switch faster and operate at higher voltage and temperature than conventional silicon,
cutting conversion losses and reaching efficiencies close to 99 percent.

• Smart-grid and HVDC integration: Power-electronic interfaces allow bidirectional power
flow, voltage regulation, and grid-stability support as more variable renewable capacity is
added, giving grid operators far more flexible control over voltage, frequency, and power
flow.

• Electrification of transport: Traction inverters, on-board chargers, battery-management
systems, and DC-DC converters manage power flow between batteries, motors, and
charging systems in electric, hybrid, and increasingly electric-aircraft applications.

• Green industry and motor drives: Variable-frequency drives (VFDs) and high-efficiency
converters   minimise   conversion   losses   and   cut   unnecessary   energy   consumption   in
industrial motors, pumps, and compressors.

• Energy storage and microgrids: Bidirectional converters regulate battery charging and
discharging, supporting grid stability, DC microgrids for rural electrification, and islanded
microgrid operation during disturbances.

Peak Demand, Supply, and Load-Shedding

Despite this installed and derated capacity, actual peak generation is frequently constrained by
fuel shortages and maintenance outages, producing a persistent gap between demand and
supply. On 12 August 2026, the national evening-peak demand reached 18,106 MW against a
delivered supply of only 15,568 MW, leaving a load-shed of 2,538 MW distributed unevenly
across the country's nine power-distribution zones, with Dhaka, Mymensingh, and Rajshahi
bearing the largest shortfalls.

Future Prospect

The role of power electronics in the energy sector is expected to expand considerably over the
coming decade, closely linked to the global transition toward clean energy and sustainable
development:

• Wider adoption of wide-bandgap devices: SiC and GaN devices are expected to see
significantly increased use as manufacturing costs fall, improving efficiency in solar
inverters, electric vehicles, industrial drives, and data centres.

• Next-generation semiconductor materials: Ultra-wide-bandgap materials such as gallium
oxide (Ga2O3) and diamond-based semiconductors are being investigated for future high-
voltage, high-temperature applications.

• Grid-forming   inverters   and   digitalisation:   Grid-forming   inverters,   solid-state
transformers,   artificial   intelligence,   and   predictive   maintenance   are   emerging   as   key
enablers for power systems with very high shares of renewable generation.

• Electric-vehicle charging infrastructure: Advanced SiC- and GaN-based converters are
expected to improve charging speed, reduce energy losses, and enhance the performance
of fast-charging stations.

• Battery storage and vehicle-to-grid systems: Wider deployment of grid-scale storage
converters and vehicle-to-grid systems is expected to further support the integration of
variable renewable generation.

• Green hydrogen and Power-to-X technologies: High-efficiency electrolyzers that convert
renewable electricity into green hydrogen will rely on power-electronic converters to help
decarbonise sectors where direct electrification is difficult, alongside continued growth of
DC microgrids for rural electrification.

Bangladesh Perspective

Bangladesh's power sector remains heavily dependent on imported natural gas, furnace oil, and
coal. Power electronics has nonetheless already enabled significant progress through the Solar
Home System (SHS) programme, one of the world's largest off-grid solar initiatives, which
uses charge controllers, DC-DC converters, and small inverters to electrify millions of rural
households without access to the national grid.

The draft Renewable Energy Policy 2025 targets 20 percent renewable electricity by 2030 and
30 percent by 2041, requiring an estimated investment of USD 35-42.6 billion between 2025
and 2040. Key challenges include limited grid infrastructure and land-acquisition delays, heavy
import-dependence for inverters and converters, outstanding payments exceeding USD 2.2
billion to independent power producers, and a shortage of skilled power-electronics engineers.
Strengthening local manufacturing, MPPT-based solar systems, grid-connected converters, and
technical education will be critical to meeting these targets while improving grid reliability and
energy security. As Bangladesh's renewable-energy targets and planned solar parks come
online, and as the national grid modernises toward smart-grid operation, power-electronic
converters will play an increasingly central role in closing the demand-supply gap sustainably
rather than through further fossil-fuel capacity additions.

Conclusion

Power electronics underpins nearly every pathway toward a more sustainable energy system,
from grid-scale renewable integration and smart-grid control to electric transport, industrial
efficiency, and energy storage. Wide-bandgap devices such as SiC and GaN now achieve
conversion efficiencies exceeding 98 percent, and the global market for power electronics in
renewable energy continues to expand steadily.

Bangladesh-specific data analysed in this report show that demand continues to rise, fossil fuels
still dominate generation, and renewable energy - while growing - remains a modest share of
the total mix at under 4 percent of grid electricity. Where natural gas supplies almost half of
total energy and evening-peak load-shedding still reaches over 2,500 MW, wider deployment
of efficient power-electronic converters, together with continued investment in solar, wind,
storage, and grid-interconnection capacity, offers one of the most practical routes to closing the
demand-supply   gap   sustainably.   Continued   research,   policy   support,   and   infrastructure
investment - alongside strengthened local technical capability - will be essential for achieving

both energy security and environmental sustainability in Bangladesh and globally in the years
ahead.
